Vijay's work addresses how current-driven spin-orbit torques propel topological spin textures in confined magnetic nanostructures. In conventional ferromagnetic tracks, magnetic skyrmions suffer from the skyrmion Hall effect—a transverse gyrotropic deflection that drives them toward device boundaries, causing velocity loss and edge annihilation. Synthetic ferrimagnets (SFiMs), composed of antiferromagnetically coupled layers with unequal moments, provide an experimentally accessible platform where the degree of magnetic compensation can be tuned to suppress this unwanted deflection while retaining electrical readability.

He employs GPU-accelerated micromagnetic simulations (using MuMax³) integrated with analytical collective-coordinate modeling (the Thiele formalism) and force-balance calculations, benchmarking numerical models against experimentally parameterized multilayers and magnetic force microscopy.

Focusing on current-driven skyrmion transport in synthetic ferrimagnetic nanotracks in the sub-100 nm regime, his work demonstrates that partial compensation suppresses the net gyrotropic force, significantly boosting longitudinal mobility while curbing transverse deflection. Below a critical driving threshold, the balance between current driving forces and boundary repulsion stabilises steady edge-confined propagation, whereas excessive driving induces annihilation. By mapping velocity and Hall angle across skyrmion sizes and multi-skyrmion configurations, his calculations identify an intermediate diameter window where high mobility and boundary confinement are simultaneously optimal for low-dissipation racetrack devices.
